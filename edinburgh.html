⚠️  The 07:00 flight on Saturday governs this whole leg

Everything below is built backwards from a 07:00 departure from Edinburgh Airport on Saturday 17 October. That means being at the terminal by about 05:15, which in turn means either a 04:00 alarm and a pre-booked taxi from the Old Town, or — the recommendation — moving to an airport hotel after dinner on the Friday. It is the single biggest structural decision on this page, so it has an appendix of its own: The last night →

The reason it matters more than a normal early flight: the morning of the 17th is not just a travel morning, it is the Irish border. She presents at Cork immigration under the Short Stay Visa Waiver Programme at around half eight, having to be calm, clear and organised with a folder of documents. Doing that on three hours’ sleep is a bad trade for one extra evening in a city-centre bed.

The flight is now booked and the time is fixed: Ryanair FR3732, EDI 07:05 to ORK 08:25, reference AV8L9S, both travellers on the one booking. The Friday below is timed to it.

📅  Two things to check this week

1. The Scottish school October break. Edinburgh’s schools usually take their October holiday in exactly this middle-of-the-month week, and if 2026 follows the pattern the city will be busier and dearer than a normal October, with the good mid-range rooms going first. Verify the City of Edinburgh Council holiday dates, then book the beds accordingly. York is unaffected — English half term is later in the month.

2. The baggage on the Cork flight — booked, but smaller than Qatar. A 20kg checked bag each went on at booking, which is the right call, but she arrived from Manila with 25kg and she is moving to Blarney until January. Weigh her bag on the Thursday, not at the desk on the Saturday: Ryanair sells extra weight far more cheaply in advance than at the airport, and an overweight bag at six in the morning on border day is the last thing you want.

Day 11
Arrival, and a Hill at Sunset
Wednesday, 14 October 2026  ·  In from York mid-afternoon
Half a day, and one hill. The train gets in mid-afternoon, so today is check in, drop everything, and go straight up Calton Hill for sunset at about a quarter past six — which hands you the entire city in one look before you have walked a step of it. Nothing is booked, nothing is timed, and dinner is wherever you fancy. Tomorrow is the big day.
Mid-afternoon — out of Waverley

🚆  Waverley → the Royal Mile

🚂
Edinburgh Waverley is in a hole, and that is the whole trick to it The station sits at the bottom of a valley between the Old Town on one side and the New Town on the other, so you do not walk out of it, you climb out of it. There are three ways up and they go to different places: Market Street (Old Town, the low end), the Waverley Steps up to Princes Street (New Town, shops, trams), and the ramp up to the Royal Mile at the top. For anywhere on the Royal Mile, follow signs to Market Street and go up Cockburn Street — a curling Victorian street of record shops that delivers you onto the High Street. About eight minutes with bags, and it is uphill the whole way. This is your first taste of the fact that Edinburgh is a city of stairs. If the bags are heavy or it is raining hard, there is a taxi rank inside the station on Waverley Bridge. Five minutes and about £8 to anywhere in the Old Town.
Sunset, about 6:15pm — the best free hour in Edinburgh
⛰  Hill & monuments, free
Calton Hill
📍  Calton Hill, Edinburgh EH7 5AA  ·  15 minutes on foot from Waverley
Free 🕐  Open all hours ⏱  An hour, including the climb

Walk east along Princes Street and keep going onto Waterloo Place; about two minutes past the old Royal High School there is a wide stone staircase up on the left. Five minutes of steps and you are on a low, flat summit covered in improbable neoclassical monuments — including the National Monument, a half-finished Parthenon abandoned in 1829 when the money ran out, which Edinburgh has been quietly embarrassed about ever since.

  • You see everything at once. The whole Old Town skyline along the ridge with the castle at the end of it, Arthur’s Seat and Salisbury Crags to the south, the New Town’s grid below, and the Firth of Forth and Fife beyond. This is the postcard, and it costs nothing.
  • Go up before sunset, not after. Sunset is about 6:15 in mid-October; be up there by six. The good half hour is the one after the sun goes, when the castle floodlights come on and the sky is still blue.
  • The Dugald Stewart Monument, the little round temple on the western edge, is the exact spot every photograph of Edinburgh is taken from. It frames the Old Town perfectly.
  • The Nelson Monument, the upturned telescope, can be climbed for a few pounds during the day — not needed, the hill itself is high enough.
Wear the coat you would wear on a boat. It is only 103 metres but it is completely exposed and the wind comes straight off the North Sea. This is the single most reliably freezing hour of the trip and it is worth being dressed for, because you will want to stay up there longer than you expect.

🆎  Dinner — warm, casual, nothing booked
Makars Gourmet Mash Bar, Bank Street, at the top of the Mound. Haggis, neeps and tatties done properly and without irony, plus about nine kinds of mash. Cheap, warm, filling, and the correct first meal in Scotland. Ten minutes down from Calton Hill.
The Cafe Royal, 19 West Register Street. A Victorian oyster bar two minutes from Calton Hill — marble, stained glass, tiled portraits of inventors, and a circular island bar. Go for the room even if you only have a drink. Food is decent pub-plus.
Dishoom, 3a St Andrew Square. Bombay-Irani cafe in a beautiful room, five minutes from the hill. It does not take small bookings in the evening and the queue can be an hour — go at half five or not at all.
The Bow Bar, 80 West Bow. Not dinner — no food to speak of — but if you want one whisky in a proper Edinburgh bar with three hundred of them on the wall and nobody playing music, this is the one.
Day 12
Castle to Summit, All Downhill
Thursday, 15 October 2026  ·  The big day — and it needs no transport at all
The whole day runs down one street. You start at the top of the Royal Mile at the castle gates, walk its length losing height the entire way, and finish by climbing the volcano at the bottom of it. No backtracking, no buses, no tickets except the first one. Two things are booked in advance: the 9:30 castle slot and dinner. Everything in between is a wander with a direction.
9:30am, at the doors — the only quiet window
🏰  Fortress, ticketed & timed
Edinburgh Castle
📍  Castlehill, Edinburgh EH1 2NG  ·  12 minutes up the Royal Mile
💰  About £20–22 each, cheaper booked direct 🕐  9:30–17:00 in October, last entry 16:00 ⏱  Two hours

Go at the doors rather than later. 9:30 to about 11:00 is the only quiet window of the day, and in a Scottish school holiday week that matters more than usual. The castle sits on a volcanic plug 130-odd metres above the city, and the thing to understand before you go in is that it is a fortress, not a palace — gun batteries, a garrison, a war memorial and a prison, arranged up a rock. It is a genuinely different animal from the Tower of London, which you have already done.

  • St Margaret’s Chapel — a single tiny room from about 1130, and the oldest building in Edinburgh. Twenty seconds to walk through and the best thing up there.
  • The Honours of Scotland in the Crown Room: the crown, sceptre and sword of state, the oldest crown jewels in Britain. Worth checking they are on display — the Crown Room has had closures for refurbishment.
  • The Stone of Destiny is no longer here. It moved to the new Perth Museum in 2024 and it is not coming back. Most guidebooks and half the internet still say otherwise. Do not go looking for it.
  • The Scottish National War Memorial is the one people walk past and the one that lands hardest. Silent, and no photography.
  • The views from the Argyle and Mills Mount batteries are half the ticket — straight down over the New Town to the Forth.
Book direct with Historic Environment Scotland. It is cheaper than the gate, it guarantees the time slot, and 9:30 slots in a holiday week do go. Resellers charge a premium for the same ticket.
The One O’Clock Gun is fired from the Mills Mount battery at 13:00 every day except Sunday. It is extremely loud and everybody jumps, including the people who knew it was coming. If you are still up there at one, stand where you can see the crowd rather than the gun.

All downhill
Late morning — off the Mile and down the hill
🏠  Streets & a graveyard, free
Victoria Street, the Grassmarket & Greyfriars Kirkyard
📍  West Bow / Victoria Street, Edinburgh EH1 2HE  ·  5 minutes below the Lawnmarket
Free ⏱  An hour and a half, wandering

A hundred metres down the Lawnmarket, turn right and drop down Victoria Street — a curved terrace of brightly painted shopfronts on a steep bend, with a raised walkway above the shops on one side. It sweeps down into the Grassmarket, a wide square under the south face of the castle rock that spent several centuries as the place they hanged people and now spends its time as the place they serve lunch.

  • The Vennel, a narrow flight of steps off the west end of the Grassmarket, gives the single best photograph of the castle in the city — the whole rock framed between tenement walls. Two minutes, and almost everybody misses it.
  • Greyfriars Kirkyard, up Candlemaker Row from the Grassmarket — a beautiful, faintly sinister 16th-century graveyard on a slope, full of table tombs and mort-safes. Free. Bobby the dog is at the gate; the “Tom Riddell” headstone is deeper in, if that is of interest.
  • Oink on Victoria Street does hog roast rolls for about six quid and is the correct cheap lunch if you would rather not sit down.
  • Mary’s Milk Bar in the Grassmarket makes gelato in small batches in front of you. There is often a queue and it is usually worth it.

Lunch — take your time, the afternoon is a climb
🍳  Somewhere in the Grassmarket or back up on the Mile
The Last Drop or The White Hart Inn, Grassmarket. Old pubs with proper lunches, both trading on being extremely old and both fine for it. The White Hart claims 1516.
Oink, Victoria Street. A hog roast roll, eaten on a wall. Six pounds, five minutes, and you have the whole afternoon back.
The Piemaker, South Bridge, if you are heading down past the museum. Exactly what it says, and about four pounds.
Clarinda’s Tea Room, 69 Canongate — near the bottom of the Mile, small and old-fashioned, and the last decent stop before Holyrood if you are running late.
Early afternoon — keep walking down
⛪  The rest of the Mile, mostly free
St Giles’, the closes, and down to Holyrood
📍  High Street, Edinburgh EH1 1RE  ·  The middle third of the Royal Mile
Free  (small charge to photograph inside St Giles’) ⏱  An hour, drifting

The stretch between the Grassmarket and Holyrood is the part to do slowly rather than tick off. St Giles’ Cathedral is free to enter and worth twenty minutes for the Thistle Chapel alone — a tiny 1911 chapel with carved stalls, absurd amounts of detail, and a stone angel playing the bagpipes.

  • The closes are the point of the Royal Mile. Duck down any of them — Advocate’s Close, Anchor Close, Fleshmarket Close — and you drop into stepped alleys between seven-storey tenements with the New Town suddenly visible at the bottom. They are free, they are everywhere, and they are more Edinburgh than any of the attractions.
  • The Real Mary King’s Close (about £23, timed, book ahead) takes you into sealed-off closes underneath the City Chambers. Genuinely good, an hour long, and the obvious rainy-day substitute for Arthur’s Seat.
  • Gladstone’s Land on the Lawnmarket — a 17th-century tenement restored floor by floor, about £10, and a rare chance to see how the Old Town actually lived.
  • Holyrood Palace is at the bottom (about £20). Worth it if you want the interiors and Mary Queen of Scots’ chambers — but it is two hours you have earmarked for a volcano, so treat it as an either/or.

Afternoon, from about 2:30 — the volcano
⛰  Extinct volcano, free
Arthur’s Seat
📍  Holyrood Park, Edinburgh EH8 8AZ  ·  Start from the car park beside Holyrood Palace
Free ⏱  45 min up, 2 hrs round trip 🕑  251m — be off the hill by 5pm

The Royal Mile delivers you to the foot of it, which is the whole reason the day is built this way. Arthur’s Seat is an extinct volcano sitting in a public park in the middle of a capital city, 251 metres up, and from the top you get Edinburgh, the Forth, Fife, and on a clear day the Pentlands and the Bass Rock. Afternoon is genuinely better than morning here: by three o’clock in October the light has gone low and gold across the whole city, and you are walking up into it rather than out of it.

  • Take the eastern route, not the crags. From the Holyrood car park, follow the path around to the left and up through the valley — the long, gradual approach known as the Dry Dam and Piper’s Walk. It is the gentlest way and the easiest to follow.
  • The Radical Road under Salisbury Crags is closed and has been for years because of rockfall. Do not plan a route along it and ignore any guidebook that suggests it — the barriers are real.
  • The last section is bare rock and it is slick when wet and genuinely windy at the top. Boots or decent trainers, not the shoes you wore for dinner. There is no shame in stopping at the false summit or at the top of the crags — the view is 90% as good.
  • Coming down the far side brings you out at Duddingston, where the Sheep Heid Inn has been serving since the 1360s and has a skittle alley in the back. It is a lovely finish, and it puts you 30 minutes and a bus ride from the Old Town rather than 10 minutes — a good trade if you are not in a hurry, a bad one if dinner is at seven.
Sunset is about 6:15, so be starting down by 5:00 at the latest. There is no lighting in Holyrood Park, the ground is uneven, and coming off a rocky summit in the dark is how people get hurt on this hill every year. Take a phone with charge.

Evening — the one dinner you book
🍸  Dinner, booked in advance  ·  the pick
The Devil’s Advocate
📍  9 Advocate’s Close, 357 High Street, Edinburgh EH1 1PS
💰  About £25–30 a main 🕐  Book now — a Thursday in the holiday week will fill ⏱  Two hours

Down a steep close off the Royal Mile, in a converted Victorian pump house — bare stone walls, low light, a mezzanine dining room above a bar carrying several hundred whiskies. It is intimate rather than grand, which after Le Café du Marché in London is the right second act: the same idea — a hidden room down an alley — done in an entirely different accent. Scottish produce, cooked properly, without ceremony.

Getting there from Holyrood: walk back up the Royal Mile, which is about 20 minutes uphill, or take the number 35 bus if the legs have gone. Advocate’s Close is on the north side of the High Street just below St Giles’ — easy to walk past, so look for the archway.

🍷  If you would rather go bigger, or smaller
Bigger — The Witchery by the Castle, Castlehill. The most theatrical dining room in Scotland: oak panelling, tapestries, candles, and the Secret Garden room downstairs. Roughly £50–70 a head and worth every penny for the room alone. Thirty seconds from the castle gates, so it can also be lunch. Books out weeks ahead.

Smaller — The Scran & Scallie, Stockbridge. Tom Kitchin’s gastropub — excellent food, no formality, and a twenty-minute walk or a short bus out of the Old Town.
Smaller — Howies, Victoria Street. Reliable Scottish cooking at a set price, five minutes from everything, and it can usually take a table at short notice.

✎  If that is too much for one day

It is a full day — a two-hour fortress, a mile of street and a 251-metre climb — and this is meant to be a holiday. Two clean ways to lighten it, both of which cost you nothing structurally.

Drop Arthur’s Seat. Calton Hill on Wednesday has already given you the view from above. Replace it with the Water of Leith walkway from Stockbridge to Dean Village — flat, about an hour, a wooded gorge and a cluster of old mill buildings hidden below the New Town, and one of the prettiest corners of any British city. Or the National Museum of Scotland, free, ten minutes away, with a roof terrace.

Or drop the castle. You have already done the Tower of London this fortnight, and if two big ticketed fortresses in ten days is one too many, this is the honest place to save the money. Surgeons’ Hall Museums on Nicolson Street — Edinburgh’s anatomy and pathology collection, and the sibling of the Hunterian you already visited — would fill the morning for a third of the price and none of the crowds. Check its opening days first; they vary by season.

🏖  Seaside town, half-day trip  ·  the pick if it is fine
North Berwick
📍  Trains from Edinburgh Waverley  ·  roughly every half hour, 33 minutes
💰  About £8–10 return each ⏱  Out at 9:30, back by 15:00 🚶  Station to the beach: 10 minutes

A proper little Scottish seaside town at the end of a branch line — a working harbour, two sandy bays either side of it, a high street of independent shops, and the sea on three sides. The station is five minutes from the high street and ten from the beach, so there is no faff at the other end. It is the sort of place that makes the last day of a trip feel like a holiday rather than a countdown.

  • The Scottish Seabird Centre at the harbour (about £11) has live remote cameras trained on Bass Rock, the white volcanic island a mile offshore that holds one of the world’s largest northern gannet colonies. You drive the cameras yourself. It sounds underwhelming and it is not.
  • North Berwick Law — a 187-metre volcanic cone behind the town, half an hour up. From the top: the whole Firth of Forth, Bass Rock, Tantallon Castle on its cliff, and Arthur’s Seat as a bump on the western horizon where you were standing yesterday. There is a whale jawbone at the summit. Only if the legs are willing — you climbed a volcano yesterday.
  • Lunch by the water at the Rocketeer or the Lobster Shack by the harbour, then coffee at Steampunk on the high street.
  • Get the 14:00-ish train back at the latest. Do not push this — the whole point of choosing a half-day is arriving back with the afternoon intact.

🛣  Village & bridges The shorter sea day
South Queensferry & the Forth Bridge
📍  Train to Dalmeny, then 10 minutes downhill  ·  about 20 minutes from Waverley
💰  About £6–8 return each ⏱  Out at 10, back for lunch or just after

Twenty minutes on a train and you are standing on a cobbled village street on the shore of the Forth, with the Forth Bridge — the 1890 cantilever railway bridge, a UNESCO World Heritage Site and probably the most recognisable piece of engineering in Scotland — going straight over your head in red steel. Three bridges, one after another, span the estuary here across a century and a half of engineering. It is a genuinely astonishing thing to stand under and it takes almost no effort to reach.

  • The Hawes Inn, right under the bridge, is where Robert Louis Stevenson wrote part of Kidnapped — and it is exactly where you want lunch.
  • Walk east along the shore under the bridge for the best view of all three spans at once.
  • This is the right choice if Thursday was hard on the legs. It is a stroll, not an expedition, and you are back in the city by early afternoon with hours in hand.

🏛  Museum, free The wet version
National Museum of Scotland
📍  Chambers Street, Edinburgh EH1 1JF  ·  10 minutes from the Royal Mile
Free ⏱  Two to three hours

Free, enormous, and one of the best museums in Britain — a Victorian glass-roofed grand gallery joined onto a modern sandstone building, covering Scottish history, natural history, science and world cultures without ever being boring. Dolly the sheep is here. So is the Lewis Chessmen hoard. And the roof terrace on the seventh floor is free, almost always empty, and gives one of the best views in the city — castle, Old Town, Salisbury Crags, all at once.

Nearby, and also indoors: the Scottish National Gallery on the Mound (free), Surgeons’ Hall Museums five minutes east, and the Real Mary King’s Close, which is underground and therefore weatherproof by definition.

16:30 — the folder hour, and it is not optional

📁  Build the Cork folder tonight, not at the airport

The Border Management Unit’s advice was specific: carry “copies of her airlines tickets and bookings in and out of the UK as proof of travel”, and be ready to “present her E-visa digitally on her mobile phone”. Tomorrow morning there will be no time and no printer. So the last hour of the holiday is admin, and it is the most valuable hour of the week.

Print in York, not in Edinburgh. The original plan here was to ask hotel reception to run off a few pages on the Thursday — but the Old Town bed is now a Travelodge, and a Travelodge has no business centre and no reception printing. Print the whole folder at the York hotel on Monday 12 or Tuesday 13 October instead, which puts two clear days between the printer and the border rather than one. Failing that, print it in London before you check out on the 12th.

If it has to happen in Edinburgh, the fallback is a print shop rather than a hotel desk — there are several around the Bridges and the University, five minutes from St Mary’s Street. Sort it on the Thursday, not Friday night.

Everything goes in hand luggage. Not the hold bag. If a bag goes astray between Edinburgh and Cork, the evidence must not go with it.

Day 14
Edinburgh to Cork
Saturday, 17 October 2026  ·  FR3732, 07:05 departure — and the Irish border

✈️  This is the day the whole plan turns on

Two weeks of holiday end at a desk at Cork Airport at about half past eight in the morning. The Border Management Unit has confirmed in writing that Cork may be used for entry under the Short Stay Visa Waiver Programme — but they were equally clear that they do not pre-clear anyone, so the decision is made in the hall, by one officer, on the day.

Everything on this page has been arranged so that you arrive at that desk rested, unhurried and organised. Treat the morning as one continuous operation rather than a flight followed by a formality.

The morning, from the airport hotel
05:15
Alarm. Bags were packed last night, the folder is in the hand luggage, both phones charged overnight. Nothing to decide.
05:35
Walk to the terminal. Two to five minutes from any of the airport hotels. No taxi, no tram, no timetable.
05:45
Bag drop, at the desk. Check in online in the app the day before — Ryanair charges heavily for airport check-in and no longer accepts printed boarding passes — but the two 20kg bags still go over a staffed desk, and that is where the human interaction happens. Have the printed Irish notice and the BMU email accessible before anyone asks. Ryanair closes bag drop 40 minutes before departure: 06:25. Treat that as the real deadline, not the 07:05.
06:05
Security. Edinburgh at six on a Saturday is usually quick, but October Saturdays are a peak leisure slot and it can surprise you. This is the hour the whole airport-hotel decision buys you.
06:30
Gate. Sunrise is not until about a quarter to eight, so you take off in the dark. Sleep on the flight rather than reading the folder — she should walk off that aircraft rested, not rehearsed into a state.
07:05
FR3732 departs Edinburgh. 1hr 20min to Cork. Same time zone, so no clock change.
08:25
Land at Cork. Follow the arrivals signs to immigration. Simon goes to the desk with her, not through the EU channel ahead of her — he is part of the evidence and he holds the address and the sponsorship.
~08:30
Immigration. The full brief, the documents in order, and the answer to every question they will ask, is on the borders page. Name the January departure date rather than asking for ninety days, and put the onward ticket on the counter. Check the stamp is legible before walking away.
~09:15
On to Blarney. About 25 minutes by road from the airport. Pre-book the car or the taxi — after that morning nobody wants to work out a bus change in the arrivals hall. Then it is done, and she is home for Christmas.
🟢  What Cork buys you, and what it costs
Saturday morning at a small airport

Worth being clear-eyed about the trade you have made, because it is a real one and it is fine.

  • What you gain: you land 25 minutes from the house instead of three hours away, with no Dublin–Cork train at the end of a 5am start. On the day of a long move, that is worth a great deal.
  • What you give up: the original reason for choosing Friday the 16th was staffing — a weekday desk at a bigger airport sees waiver cases routinely. Cork is small and Saturday is quiet, so the officer may well need to check with a supervisor. That is normal and it is not a bad sign. Expect it, be patient, do not fill the silence, and have the printed BMU email on top of the folder so the answer is in the room already.
  • The deadline is intact. Entry under the programme must happen by 31 October 2026. The 17th clears that by a fortnight, which is the margin that matters if anything slips.

🔄  If the 07:00 does not fly

Edinburgh–Cork is a thin route — likely one rotation a day, and possibly not every day — so a cancellation cannot be fixed by waiting for the next one. Know the fallback before you need it, because you will be working it out at six in the morning.

The fallback is Dublin, and the border is not compromised by taking it. BMU named “either Cork or Dublin airport” in writing, so switching does not weaken anything. Edinburgh–Dublin has several flights a day on more than one airline, and from Dublin it is a train from Heuston to Cork in about two and a half hours, or a bus. Longer and more tiring, but it works, and it still lands the entry on the 17th.

And if the whole day goes: the programme deadline is 31 October. There is a two-week cushion. Rebook for the 18th or 19th rather than forcing anything, and change the Blarney arrival rather than the border.

Appendices

The last night, and the alternatives

One decision that changes the shape of Friday, and a shelf of swap-ins for the days that do not go to plan.

Appendix A
The Last Night: Airport or Old Town
Friday 16 October, and the 07:00 on Saturday

Both versions work. The recommendation is the airport, and the reason is the border rather than the flight — but it is a genuine choice and the case for staying in town is not silly.

Recommended
Airport hotel, Friday night

05:15 alarm, five-minute walk. Dinner in the Old Town at six, bags at half eight, tram at nine, in the room by ten. You keep the entire Friday and the whole evening in Edinburgh. Cost is roughly a wash: an airport room in October is usually no dearer than an Old Town one in a holiday week, and you save a £30 taxi.

The alternative
Stay in the Old Town

03:45 alarm, pre-booked taxi at 04:20, at the terminal by about 04:50. Do not rely on the tram: its first departure of the day is too late and too tight for a 07:00 flight. The Airlink 100 bus from Waverley Bridge runs through the night and is the reliable public option — about 30 minutes — but with three months of luggage a booked taxi is worth the money.

⚖️  What actually decides it

It is not the flight, it is what happens ninety minutes after the flight. A 4am start is survivable for anyone; people do it all the time. But at half past eight she has to stand at an immigration desk and be calm, clear, warm and organised in a conversation that determines whether the next three months happen. Sleep is not a luxury on that morning, it is preparation.

The second consideration is luggage. She is not going home after this — she is moving to Blarney until January, and everything she owns for that period is coming with her. Dragging that out of a city-centre hotel into a taxi at twenty past four in the dark, then out again at the terminal, is a materially worse morning than wheeling it across a car park at twenty to six.

Stay in town if: you find airport hotels genuinely depressing and the last night in a real city matters more to you than an hour and a half of sleep. That is a legitimate answer — just book the taxi the day before, and confirm it the night before.

Appendix B
Swap-ins
For rain, tired legs, or a change of mind
🌴  River walk, free
Water of Leith & Dean Village
📍  Stockbridge to Dean Village  ·  20 minutes from the Old Town
Free ⏱  An hour, flat

The best flat hour in Edinburgh, and the natural replacement for Arthur’s Seat if the legs have gone. A wooded river gorge running underneath the New Town, coming out at Dean Village — a cluster of old mill buildings and a millpond in a hollow, five minutes from Princes Street and completely hidden from it. Carry on to St Bernard’s Well, a little domed Roman temple over a mineral spring. It is genuinely lovely in the rain.

⚓  Ship, ticketed
The Royal Yacht Britannia
📍  Ocean Terminal, Leith EH6 6JJ  ·  25 minutes by bus from Princes Street
💰  About £20 ⏱  Two to three hours including the journey

The royal family’s floating home for forty years, kept exactly as it was — an audio tour through five decks, from the state apartments to the engine room, and the crew’s quarters are as interesting as the royal ones. Indoors, warm, and unaffected by weather. It fills a wet Friday morning better than a museum does because it is a completely different kind of thing.

It eats most of a morning once you count Leith and back, so it does not combine with a day trip. Take it instead of North Berwick, not as well as.

⛪  Chapel, ticketed
Rosslyn Chapel
📍  Roslin, Midlothian EH25 9PU  ·  About 50 minutes on the Lothian 37 bus
💰  About £12 ⏱  Half a day with the travel

A small 15th-century chapel carved to an almost hallucinatory degree — every surface, inside and out, covered in foliage, angels, dragons and the famous Apprentice Pillar. Famous now for the wrong reasons, and worth going for the right ones: it is genuinely unlike any other building in Britain. The bus goes from the city centre and takes about fifty minutes, which is the only real objection.

🧪  Anatomy museum, ticketed
Surgeons’ Hall Museums
📍  Nicolson Street, Edinburgh EH8 9DW  ·  10 minutes from the Royal Mile
💰  About £10 ⏱  90 minutes

The sibling of the Hunterian you saw in London: one of the largest surgical pathology collections in the world, plus the history of surgery in Edinburgh and a room on Burke and Hare that is exactly as grim as you would hope. The obvious substitute if you decide to skip Edinburgh Castle — a third of the price, none of the crowds, and a direct follow-on from something you have already enjoyed. Check the opening days before you go; they vary with the season.

🌲  Botanic garden, grounds free
Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh
📍  Arboretum Place, Edinburgh EH3 5NZ  ·  20 minutes from the city centre
Grounds free  glasshouses ticketed ⏱  Two hours

Seventy acres, free to walk, with a famous view back to the Old Town skyline across the lawn.
